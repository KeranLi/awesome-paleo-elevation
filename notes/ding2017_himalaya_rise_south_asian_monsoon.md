# Quantifying the rise of the Himalaya orogen and implications for the South Asian monsoon

**Paper:** Ding, L., Spicer, R. A., Yang, J., Xu, Q., Cai, F., Li, S., et al. (2017). *Geology*. [10.1130/G38583.1](https://doi.org/10.1130/G38583.1)

**Status:** Read on 2026-08-30.

## Question and study area

The study quantifies the rise of the proto-Himalaya and considers consequences for the South Asian monsoon. It uses the Liuqu and Qiabulin basins in the central Himalayan region to construct a localized paleoelevation history and assess whether it records wider uplift along the southern flank of Tibet.

## Evidence and method

The reconstruction uses fossil floras and Climate Leaf Analysis Multivariate Program (CLAMP) results, together with stratigraphic context and comparison with published stable-isotope estimates. The floras are interpreted in relation to modern and coeval lowland vegetation to constrain paleoclimate and elevation.

## Paleoelevation history

The approximately 56 Ma Liuqu flora contains tropical and subtropical vegetation, including palms, resembling roughly coeval near-sea-level floras in India and southern China. CLAMP results and the palm-rich tropical assemblage indicate that Liuqu was still relatively low, at approximately **1 km or lower**, soon after deposition of the final marine units.

By the earliest Miocene, the Qiabulin flora lacks palms and is temperate, unlike palm-rich near-coeval coastal vegetation in northern India. The study estimates an elevation of approximately **2.3 km**. Published stable-isotope evidence places the Mount Everest region above **5.0 km by 15 Ma** (Gébelin et al., 2013), comparable with contemporaneous elevation estimates from Namling.

Together, the record supports substantial Himalayan growth after the early Miocene. The authors argue that this localized elevation history may reflect uplift and growing convergence resistance along a wider sector of Tibet's southern flank, rather than a purely local event.

## South Asian monsoon implication

Climate modelling indicates that a high Himalayan barrier can be more important for modern South Asian monsoon circulation than the height or extent of the entire plateau (Boos and Kuang, 2010). The study nevertheless finds evidence for monsoon-like rainfall seasonality south of the Himalaya-Tibet edifice since the Paleogene.

Early Eocene leaves from Gurha, India (approximately 55-52 Ma) indicate a strong seasonal rainfall contrast consistent with monsoonal conditions (Shukla et al., 2014). At low paleolatitudes, such seasonality can arise from seasonal ITCZ migration. However, seasonal-rainfall evidence poleward of the ITCZ migration range, including ostracod δ18O variation in the high Gangdese Mountains, suggests that the proto-Tibetan Plateau and Gangdese topography also helped shape the late Paleocene-early Eocene South Asian monsoon (Licht et al., 2014; Ding et al., 2014).

## Limitations

- The primary elevation evidence is localized to the Liuqu and Qiabulin basins; extension to the whole Himalayan chain is a regional interpretation.
- Floral and CLAMP estimates depend on the climate-elevation calibration and on comparison with appropriate lowland floras.
- Monsoon-like rainfall seasonality is not uniquely diagnostic of the modern South Asian monsoon; ITCZ migration, palaeogeography, and topography can all contribute.
- The >5 km estimate at 15 Ma comes from a separate stable-isotope study and should be evaluated with its own proxy limitations.

## Maintainer interpretation

This paper provides an important transition from a low proto-Himalaya near 56 Ma to a higher, temperate central Himalayan landscape in the early Miocene. It also argues that monsoon-like seasonality predated the high modern Himalaya, while later Himalayan growth likely changed the strength and spatial expression of the South Asian monsoon.
