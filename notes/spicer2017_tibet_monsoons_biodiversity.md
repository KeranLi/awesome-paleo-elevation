# Tibet, the Himalaya, Asian monsoons and biodiversity

**Paper:** Spicer, R. A. (2017). *Plant Diversity*. [10.1016/j.pld.2017.09.001](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.pld.2017.09.001)

**Status:** Read on 2026-08-29.

## Scope

This review examines proposed links among Tibetan and Himalayan topography, Asian monsoon evolution, and the diversification of Asian biota over roughly the last 60 million years. It contrasts the traditional view of a Neogene, collision-driven uplift and monsoon onset with evidence for older highlands and older seasonal rainfall.

## Topographic evolution

The review argues that a proto-Tibetan highland existed before the Neogene. It discusses evidence for Andean-type relief, with parts of Tibet reaching at least about 4.5 km near the beginning of the Eocene, before final Tethys closure and the India-Asia collision. Other syntheses indicate that parts of Tibet were already above 3 km between approximately 45 and 30 Ma (Burg and Chen, 1984; Dewey et al., 1989; England and Searle, 1986; Murphy et al., 1997).

The staged model summarized by Wang et al. (2014) places an elevated Himalaya at 45-35 Ma, elevated southern Tibet by about 25 Ma, and progressive northeastward development of the plateau. In that model, most of the plateau except the northeast was close to its present mean surface elevation by about 15 Ma. Other evidence discussed in the review suggests that the high Himalaya itself may have developed relatively late: isotopic studies indicate near-modern Himalayan elevations within the last 15 Ma, while Mount Everest may have reached about 5 km by 15 Ma and risen a further roughly 3 km afterward (Garzione et al., 2000; Rowley et al., 2001; Saylor et al., 2009; Gebelin et al., 2013).

The review emphasizes that basin-floor elevation estimates can be uncertain because carbonate and water isotopes may record evaporation, repeated precipitation-re-evaporation, or an integrated catchment rather than local altitude. Fossil evidence from the Lunpola Basin has been interpreted as indicating a surface perhaps 2 km lower than some isotope-based estimates (Deng et al., 2011; Sun et al., 2014; Polissar et al., 2009; Rowley and Currie, 2006).

## Monsoon mechanisms

Modern Asian monsoons are not necessarily driven by one mechanism. ITCZ migration provides a low-latitude seasonal rainfall framework, while land-sea thermal contrast, mountain barriers, plateau heating, and changing moisture pathways modify the regional circulation. Boos and Kuang (2010) found that flattening both the Himalaya and Tibet weakens the South Asian Monsoon (SAM), whereas retaining the Himalaya alone preserves much of the modern SAM structure. Molnar et al. (2010) therefore emphasized the Himalayan barrier: by blocking northern ventilation, it helps maintain the hot low-pressure cell over northwestern India and Pakistan that draws moist air from the Indian Ocean.

The review also stresses that a marked wet-dry seasonal ratio is not by itself proof of a monsoon. Geological proxies deposited in basins may be buffered or biased by water accumulation, and non-monsoonal Mediterranean-type climates can also be strongly seasonal. Meteorological definitions should consider rainfall amount, season onset and cessation, and atmospheric pressure (Wang and Fan, 1999; Wang and Ho, 2002; Zhang and Wang, 2008).

## Ancient monsoons and climate context

Because seasonal ITCZ migration is a consequence of Earth's obliquity, some form of low-latitude monsoon climate could have existed throughout much of Earth history. Its intensity and geographic expression would have changed with palaeogeography, ocean currents, topography, the equator-to-pole temperature gradient, Hadley circulation, and ice volume. Eocene climate models produce pronounced monsoonal conditions in southern Asia even with Tibet flattened (Huber and Goldner, 2012), while proxy and modelling studies support ancient seasonal rainfall (Shukla et al., 2014; Licht et al., 2014). These records do not by themselves establish whether the ancient system was a simple ITCZ monsoon or a topographically modified analogue of the modern SAM.

## Biodiversity implication

The review challenges the claim that Asian biodiversity and widespread Miocene speciation began only after a single Neogene uplift of the whole plateau. It instead places the origins of high Asian biodiversity mainly in the Paleogene and proposes that later, spatially uneven uplift and monsoon reorganization modified existing ecological gradients. The key causal question is therefore not simply whether Tibet was high, but which sector rose, when it rose, and how that changed seasonal moisture, habitat connectivity, and ecological niche diversity.

## Maintainer interpretation

Spicer's main contribution is to separate three questions that are often merged: when high relief existed, when different components of the Asian monsoon developed, and when biodiversity increased. The review supports a long-lived, evolving monsoon framework and a multi-stage Tibetan-Himalayan topographic history, while recognizing that proxy representativeness and chronology remain major uncertainties.
