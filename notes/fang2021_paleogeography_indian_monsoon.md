# Paleogeography control of Indian monsoon intensification and expansion at 41 Ma

**Paper:** Fang, X., Yan, M., Zhang, W., Nie, J., Han, W., Wu, F., Song, C., Zhang, T., Zan, J., and Yang, Y. (2021). *Science Bulletin*. [10.1016/j.scib.2021.07.023](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.scib.2021.07.023)

**Status:** Read on 2026-09-01.

## Question and study area

The study examines why the Indian monsoon intensified and expanded northward around 41 Ma. It focuses on the paleogeographic and topographic context of southern Asia, with geological evidence from the Yunnan region and climate-model experiments involving the Tibetan-Himalayan region, the Indian Ocean, and surrounding landmasses.

## Chronology and evidence

The target interval is approximately 41 Ma. The studied section contains a Late Eocene Caijiachong fauna with abundant mammal fossils; upper tuff layers provide ages of **37.03 ± 0.36 Ma** and **35.49 ± 0.78 Ma**. The authors also discuss the transition from Paleogene clastic and evaporitic deposits to Neogene lighter-coloured strata containing coal and limestone as regional evidence for changing hydroclimate.

Fine-grained hematite is used as a dry-climate indicator because hematite acts as a pigment and high concentrations can reflect oxidizing, seasonally dry conditions (Schwertmann, 1971; Zhang et al., 2007). The study also reconstructs palaeosalinity from clay-fraction boron and clay-mineral assemblages. Samples are pre-treated to remove organic matter and carbonate, the <2 μm fraction is separated using settling methods, boron is measured by ICP-MS, and XRD treatments identify illite, smectite, and kaolinite. The normalized kaolinite boron content is converted to salinity using the Couch empirical relationship.

## Main interpretation

The authors argue that the Indian monsoon may have begun in the Eocene or even the Paleocene, rather than being an exclusively Neogene consequence of Tibetan uplift. The onset age remains debated, with published proposals ranging from the Cretaceous or Paleocene-Eocene to the late Eocene and early Miocene (Huber and Goldner, 2012; Farnsworth et al., 2019; Licht et al., 2014; Clift et al., 2008, among others).

Numerical experiments cited in the paper indicate that without an elevated Tibetan Plateau, Indian monsoon rainfall remains largely confined to ITCZ-driven fluctuations near 20°N. With an elevated plateau, the monsoon circulation can extend farther north into the temperate zone (Boos and Kuang, 2010; Wu et al., 2012; Liu et al., 2019).

Around 41 Ma, uplift in northwestern Yunnan is interpreted to have supplied both topographic and thermal conditions favourable for monsoon development. The Tangula Mountains could provide a heat source and low-pressure centre that draws moisture northward from the tropical Indian Ocean. Orographic uplift then produces rainfall and latent-heat release, reinforcing ascent and the inflow of further moisture. This is a positive feedback between plateau heating, moisture transport, orographic precipitation, and latent heating (Zhu et al., 2019).

The study further proposes that Antarctic glaciation and global cooling shifted climate zones northward. Acting together with northwestern Yunnan uplift, these changes may have promoted northward expansion of the Asian monsoon and changed Yunnan from relatively dry to wetter conditions near 41 Ma.

## Limitations

- The 41 Ma interpretation combines regional sedimentary and geochemical evidence with model-based mechanisms; it is not a direct elevation reconstruction.
- Hematite, salinity, and clay-mineral signals can respond to several hydrological and diagenetic processes and should not be treated as unique monsoon indicators.
- The timing of Indian monsoon initiation differs among proxies, models, and definitions of “monsoon.”
- The relative contributions of northwestern Yunnan uplift, broader Tibetan topography, global cooling, and Antarctic ice growth remain difficult to separate.

## Maintainer interpretation

This paper supports a pre-Neogene, geographically evolving Indian monsoon and highlights 41 Ma as a possible intensification and northward-expansion interval. Its main lesson for paleo-elevation studies is that regional topography and global climate background must be evaluated together: a monsoon signal can reflect the interaction of uplift, ITCZ migration, land-sea contrast, and cryosphere-driven circulation change rather than a single plateau-uplift event.
